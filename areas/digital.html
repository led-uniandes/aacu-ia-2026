Transformación digital

La IA ya está dentro del LMS, de los sistemas licenciados y de las herramientas que profesores y estudiantes conectan por su cuenta. Las decisiones de compra, integración y permisos definen qué se puede hacer con seguridad y qué datos quedan expuestos.

Qué vimos
  • Un equipo de tres personas construyó herramientas propias con asistentes de programación: remediación de accesibilidad con cerca de 95 % menos tiempo y un tutor con más de 9.000 mensajes antes del primer parcial (Illinois State University).
  • Los agentes heredan los permisos de quien los usa, incluido lo que se comparte de más. En un LMS simulado, un agente al que se le bloqueó un conector usó el navegador para seguir actuando (Mississippi State University, Arizona State University, Georgia State University).
  • La documentación de los proveedores no permitía saber si 17 recursos licenciados entrenan con los datos o si los retienen (San José State University).
  • Una vía rápida para usos de IA de bajo riesgo y revisión profunda para los de alto riesgo, con sincronización semanal entre el equipo de IA y TI, donde el CIO y el CISO tienen veto (Metropolitan State University of Denver).
  • Modelos de lenguaje dentro de tableros institucionales: el reto principal fue mantener buenos metadatos (Virginia Tech).
Por qué le importa al área

La IA ya está dentro del LMS, de los sistemas licenciados y de las herramientas que profesores y estudiantes conectan por su cuenta. Las decisiones de compra, integración y permisos definen qué se puede hacer con seguridad y qué datos quedan expuestos.

Recomendaciones

  1. Revisar los permisos y las carpetas compartidas antes de conectar agentes o asistentes a los sistemas institucionales.
  2. Llevar preguntas estándar a cada contrato de IA: si el proveedor entrena con los datos, cuánto tiempo los guarda y cómo se sale.
  3. Crear un equipo pequeño que construya herramientas propias para procesos concretos, con una regla de mantenimiento y retiro.

Generative Trust: An Agent Evaluation Ladder for Small Teams

Miércoles 28 de octubre · 4:00 PM · 1 material (solicite acceso a SharePoint)
James Madison University
Descripción

Building AI agents has become accessible. Evaluating them has not. The generative, nondeterministic nature of these systems means the same agent can produce meaningfully different outputs across runs, and no unit test will catch that. The three-person data team within the Student Success Analytics unit at James Madison University built an AI Agent Evaluation Ladder to assess agents in production without overinvesting evaluation effort. The ladder moves from lightweight to rigorous: (1) binary yes/no checks for task completion, safety, and content accuracy; (2) rating-scale evaluations of usefulness, clarity, and alignment; (3) golden-response comparisons drawn from reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) concepts; and (4) pre/post user surveys measuring impact on training readiness and confidence. At every stage, evaluations can be run by human reviewers, LLM-based evaluators, or both. Matching evaluation depth to the maturity and risk of each use case keeps teams from overengineering low-stakes tools while building warranted trust in high-stakes ones. Working alongside an AI advisory team, the ladder has shortened evaluation cycles and supported deployment of agents for internal training, advisor meeting preparation, and similar use cases. No prior experience with AI evaluation is assumed.

Software on Demand: A Replicable Model for Purpose-Fit AI in Higher Education

Miércoles 28 de octubre · 5:00 PM · 1 material (solicite acceso a SharePoint)
Illinois State University
Descripción

Every institution faces the same AI adoption default: evaluate vendor products, negotiate a license, and renew annually. This session presents an alternative developed and deployed at Illinois State University, a regional comprehensive with no dedicated AI engineering budget, and examines the real costs and trade-offs of building versus buying. The Adaptive Edge Institute (AEI) builds purpose-fit AI tools on hybrid infrastructure the institution owns and operates. The flagship example is deck-check, an automated accessibility remediation tool that transforms PowerPoint, Word, PDF, and LaTeX files into WCAG-compliant, screen-reader-ready formats, generating context-aware alt-text, flagging reading order issues, transcribing embedded video, and auditing table markup. It processes a 40-slide presentation in under two minutes, reducing remediation time by approximately 95 percent. A second tool provides 24/7 AI tutoring across all sections of a high-DFW introductory accounting course. A third replaced months of manual administrative analysis with auditable AI-assisted workflows. AEI calls this approach “Software on Demand”: a three-person team identifies a need, co-develops the tool with the faculty or staff who will use it, deploys it, and iterates. No per-seat licensing, no vendor dependency. The institution controls the data, the design, and the decision to continue or retire a tool.

Governance as Your AI Compass: Building a Living Framework for Institutional AI Adoption

Miércoles 28 de octubre · 5:00 PM · 2 materiales (solicite acceso a SharePoint)
Mississippi State University
Descripción

Most institutions approach AI governance as a prerequisite, something that must be perfected before anyone can act. At Mississippi State University, we started that way too. In 2024, we stood up a Data Governance Committee because every best practice said governance was the way to start. We quickly learned that governance means different things to different people. To IT staff, it means tool controls, file permission, and infrastructure security. To the campus community, it means knowing what they can and cannot do. Over time, those two perspectives merged into something more useful than either one alone. This session shares how MSU developed AI governance as a continuous, community-driven process. We describe how structured input from faculty and staff, through an AI Ambassadors program and roundtable discussion, surfaced real concerns and translated them into emerging principles and practical guidelines. We share how governance committees and technology teams worked together to connect institutional guidelines to technical infrastructure. Governance is not a one-time deliverable. The questions change as tools evolve, experience deepens, and institutional needs shift. MSU treats governance as a feedback loop where the community shaping the guidelines is also testing them in practice.

The Emerging Tech Empire and Higher Education Governance

Miércoles 28 de octubre · 5:00 PM · 2 materiales (solicite acceso a SharePoint)
Learning Agenda
Descripción

Higher education institutions are adopting AI at a pace that outstrips their capacity to govern it. Institutions focus on acceptable use, academic integrity, privacy, security, and implementation. These concerns leave a larger question unaddressed. As AI becomes embedded across teaching, research, and administration, who controls and who benefits from the data, infrastructure, and knowledge on which institutions depend? The AI empire, or the colleges themselves? This session examines how AI is becoming embedded in higher education. There is a highly concentrated ecosystem where a small number of companies increasingly control the platforms through which academic work is conducted. Acting without a broader governance framework risks transferring core educational functions, and the knowledge and data generated through them, from institutions and their communities to vendors. Drawing on the Sustainable Learning Framework, the session introduces four dimensions for assessing whether AI adoption strengthens institutional sovereignty or deepens dependency. These dimensions make visible what conventional governance checklists often miss: whether institutions retain the authority and capacity to shape technology toward their educational missions, govern what it generates, and maintain meaningful alternatives. The workshop connects campus-wide governance to the agency of people working across institutional roles. Each participant will apply the four dimensions to one real AI-related decision, practice, or platform from their own work using a structured self-assessment instrument. Responses will be aggregated in real time to surface patterns across roles. Participants will leave with a practical tool, a clearer understanding of where they can exercise agency, and concrete next steps for contributing to more intentional, mission-aligned AI decisions within their institutions.

Give the People What They Want: Low-Effort Data Exploration/Visualization with Agentic AI

Miércoles 28 de octubre · 7:30 PM · 1 material (solicite acceso a SharePoint)
Swarthmore College
Descripción

Most institutions have dedicated professionals (often in offices of Institutional Research or Effectiveness) who specialize in gathering, analyzing, and displaying data. They are valued colleagues, but they are busy people (and sometimes we are impatient!). When you have a question that might be answerable with data that you already have on hand or that exists in a public place, it’s nice to be able to just analyze it yourself. But depending on the question and the data source(s), there can be obstacles: different data formats, data spread across multiple web pages, or lack of knowledge of the right tools or techniques for analyzing and displaying the data. Enter agentic AI. These new tools are very good at taking some complex or tedious tasks and making them more accessible to us, using only natural-language prompts. Often, if you can describe what you want to do, agentic tools can make it happen. I will present some examples of exploratory projects I’ve undertaken, including building a simple dashboard to find and display pairs of similar courses using descriptions gathered from the web, and a tool to scan a website for files that may need to be updated to meet accessibility standards. I will walk through how I built these to show the process, and provide more general tips for tackling projects like this. Finally, I will briefly discuss some types of analyses that may not be well suited to this approach, and when to turn to your local professionals for help.

Making AI Visible: A Framework for Documenting and Evaluating AI Across Library Databases

Jueves 29 de octubre · 11:00 AM · 1 material (solicite acceso a SharePoint)
San Jose State University
Descripción

Artificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly embedded in systems that support teaching, learning, and research across higher education, yet these systems are often poorly documented, inconsistently disclosed, or obscured by proprietary constraints. Institutions are therefore responsible for technologies they cannot fully evaluate or explain, creating challenges for transparency, governance, and accountability. This session draws on work from an academic library context and presents three strategies: institutional AI inventories to map where AI operates, structured analysis of vendor documentation to assess transparency, and internal “shadow documentation” to capture observed system behavior where disclosures are limited. These approaches offer a scalable model for making AI systems more visible and supporting informed decision-making. Participants will briefly identify an AI-enabled system in their own institution and consider what is known (and unknown) about its operation.

VOKALIX: AI Based Scalable, Online Oral Examinations

Jueves 29 de octubre · 3:00 PM · 1 material (solicite acceso a SharePoint)
The Hebrew University of Jerusalem & Vokalix In
Descripción

Online teaching has gained significant momentum over the past decade, with substantial investments made in producing high-quality courses. The most significant limitation of online courses is the lack of reliable online assessment tools. The inability to verify that an assignment or remote exam was completed by the student himself without unauthorized assistance remains a major shortcoming. This creates significant barriers to implementing both formative assessment and online exams. We will present VOKALIX, a system we developed and deployed at the Hebrew University, based on fully automated, highly versatile online oral examinations. The system uses artificial intelligence (AI) to identify the examinee and employs multiple mechanisms to ensure that the student does not rely on external tools, other people, or the internet. Our proctoring system preserves student privacy, is automated, cost-effective, and can be integrated into a variety of platforms. This stands in contrast to expensive commercial systems that rely on invasive computer monitoring, software installation, and multi-camera surveillance by human proctors. In the presentation, we will demonstrate the system and its application in online courses. This tool enables the realization of the vast potential of online learning by solving the problem of reliable remote assessment.

The Org Chart Is the Problem: What Agentic AI Is Forcing Us to Finally Fix

Jueves 29 de octubre · 5:00 PM · 1 material (solicite acceso a SharePoint)
Forsyth Technical Community College
Descripción

Think about the last student your institution lost between departments. Nobody dropped the ball on purpose. The handoff just broke. Because the structure was never built for her. It was built for us. We've been deploying AI into that same structure and expecting different results. Marketing gets a chatbot. Advising gets a nudge platform. Financial aid gets an automation layer. The org chart stays intact. The student still experiences a fragmented institution. Agentic AI changes the math. It doesn't wait for a prompt. It initiates and moves across systems on a student's behalf. When it hits a silo, it exposes it. A returning adult asking about reenrollment doesn't care whether the answer lives in advising, the registrar, or financial aid. She needs one clear path and a human she can reach if something breaks. At Forsyth Tech, we integrated marketing, recruitment, student care, and onboarding under one umbrella. What we found: the organizational model isn't a sidebar to the technology conversation. It is the technology conversation. Our chatbot saved more than 170,000 minutes of staff time. That is time that went back to advisors having harder, more human conversations. This session isn't a case study in having it figured out. It's a conversation from inside an ongoing redesign, with real data on what changed and what we'd do differently. Attendees leave with a framework for auditing their own structure, a diagnostic for finding where silos create student friction.

AI-RA: An AI-Powered Research Assistant

Jueves 29 de octubre · 5:00 PM · 1 material (solicite acceso a SharePoint)
SUNY Oswego · SUNY Oneonta
Descripción

The AI-RA project proposes to act as a catalyst to facilitate the advantageous use of AI-assisted technologies by creating and disseminating knowledge and ethics on AI-assisted tools and methods for research and creative activities across SUNY. This expertise will help build an AI Research Assistant (AI-RA) to enable researchers and students to leverage AI resources in a responsible and beneficial manner with the goal of enhancing research skills and productivity across SUNY. The project-specific aims addressed are the following: Aim 1— To create and disseminate knowledge on AI-assisted tools and methods for research and creative activities. This aim collects faculty and graduate students’ use, expectations, and concerns regarding AI for research and creative activities, first across the SUNY system, then more broadly. It also recommends optimal GenAI tools for research in a broad range of research tasks faculty and students may be interested in pursuing. Aim 2— To create a course on Enhancing Research and Creative Activities through AI-assisted Technologies . This course educates faculty members and graduate students on research and creative activities skills in the AI-powered present and future context. The course addresses many questions on AI support for specific research tasks, ethical use, intellectual property, plagiarism, cost, and challenges. Aim 3— To create and make available a personalized AI-RA chatbot built on the previously collected information and course.

Building AI Agents for Teaching and Learning: Purpose, Design, and Human Judgment

Viernes 30 de octubre · 9:00 AM · 1 material (solicite acceso a SharePoint)
College of Saint Benedict and Saint John’s University · Indeed
Descripción

As AI tools evolve from chatbots to more agentic systems, educators need practical frameworks for deciding when and how these tools should be used in teaching and learning. This session explores AI agents through three core design questions: What problem is the agent solving? What boundaries should shape its role? Where should human judgment remain central? Rather than treating AI agents as inherently innovative or inherently risky, this discussion invites participants to think critically about their pedagogical value, limitations, and implications. The session will introduce participants to AI agents and help participants consider when an AI agent is genuinely useful, when it may be unnecessary or counterproductive, and how to design for responsible human oversight. Through examples and guided discussion, participants will examine how choices about purpose, scope, and human involvement shape whether AI agents support or undermine meaningful learning. The conversation will also address broader questions of ethics, safety, accountability, bias, and the preservation of distinctly human dimensions of education such as interpretation, care, mentorship, and professional judgment. Participants will leave with a practical framework for evaluating and designing AI agents in ways that are pedagogically grounded, human-centered, and aligned with the goals of higher education.
