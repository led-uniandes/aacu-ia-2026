DIDACTA

DIDACTA ya tiene un tutor socrático, guías de IA generativa y reuniones con directores de programa. Lo visto ofrece procedimientos para escalar sin crear un currículo paralelo y para probar herramientas con rigor antes de abrirlas a los estudiantes.

Qué vimos
  • Consultores con descarga docente acompañan a cada departamento con un arranque de 2 horas y tres consultas, y con preguntas que devuelven la decisión a la disciplina (Miami University).
  • De talleres (2023) a comunidades (2024) a ecosistema (2025 en adelante). Para llegar a unos 2.000 profesores, una red de unos 50 embajadores (The Ohio State University, Clemson University).
  • Tutores probados con estudiantes simulados, y una vista para que el profesor vea las conversaciones de sus estudiantes (Macalester College, Indian River State College).
  • Plantillas de diseño universal para el aprendizaje (DUA) con IA y una lista de accesibilidad en cinco áreas; empezar por usos acotados como el texto alternativo (Virginia Tech, University of Cincinnati).
Por qué le importa al área

DIDACTA ya tiene un tutor socrático, guías de IA generativa y reuniones con directores de programa. Lo visto ofrece procedimientos para escalar sin crear un currículo paralelo y para probar herramientas con rigor antes de abrirlas a los estudiantes.

Recomendaciones

  1. Usar las reuniones con directores de programa para acordar un pedido mínimo por programa (un resultado y una secuencia de cursos), mapeando el recorrido de cada uno.
  2. Montar un banco de pruebas para el tutor socrático con estudiantes simulados y una vista para el profesor.
  3. Formar una red de embajadores por facultad para llegar más allá del equipo central.

Academic Integrity in the Age of AI: Practical Strategies to Positively Influence Cheating and Student Learning

Miércoles 28 de octubre · 8:00 AM · 1 material (solicite acceso a SharePoint)
Kansas State University · AAC&U
Descripción

While many institutions are grappling with curricular reforms to support AI literacy, a recent AAC&U survey of faculty revealed that academic integrity and cheating continue to be the primary concerns of those in the classroom. With the emergence of agentic AI, academic integrity concerns have become even more complicated. While the challenges presented by AI in terms of academic integrity are thorny, there are practical, evidence-based steps faculty can take to decrease the probability students will choose to cheat in their courses. This workshop will present the options, including effective AI detection solutions, and provide a portfolio of strategies that can positively impact students’ behaviors regarding cheating. In addition to exploring an array of strategies applicable across all disciplines, the workshop will feature hands-on activities in which participants will analyze sample assignments, use AI detection tools, explore AI tools from both student and instructor perspectives, and redesign course activities to align academic integrity goals with meaningful learning outcomes. Particular attention will be given to strategies that make cheating less attractive, learning more visible, and AI use more intentional and ethical.

AI for Inclusive Design

Miércoles 28 de octubre · 4:00 PM · 2 materiales (solicite acceso a SharePoint)
Independent Scholar
Descripción

Come learn two ways that we can incorporate generative artificial intelligence (AI) methods to lower access barriers in the design of our learning experiences. When was the last time you heard colleagues talk about how AI tools are either (a) ruining our collective ability to think and address complex challenges or (b) a miraculous solution that optimizes our time and energy, saving us from repetitive labor, low-complexity drudgery, and having to decide which shirt to wear with those slacks? Like most new tools that we’ve encountered in the past many decades in higher education, AI products are still just tools—tools that we can learn how to use well and situate in the progression of our learning activities. In this innovation session, we will expand on the universal design for learning (UDL) framework for creating learner engagements, content, and activities. Whether you are new to or familiar with UDL, you’ll discover specific actions you can take in order to lower access barriers for a variety of learners. We will also build on the foundation of UDL to discover ways to help learners to use artificial intelligence (AI) tools effectively at all stages of the learning arc, from beginners through proficiency all the way to approaching expertise. As a cap to our conversation, we’ll also note several places in the design process where we as classroom professionals can use AI tools ourselves in order to make materials and activities more engaging and more accessible for learners.

Incorporating Generative AI into Writing: A View across Three Institutions

Miércoles 28 de octubre · 4:00 PM · 2 materiales (solicite acceso a SharePoint)
University of California Irvine · Cypress College · California State University, Fullerton
Descripción

A panel of UC, CSU, and CCC instructors and researchers will discuss their efforts to integrate generative AI into writing courses without offloading necessary learning. Using a tool created at UCI to scaffold students’ AI use through instructor-mediated access, the team has spent over three years studying the incorporation of generative AI into courses, instructor and student challenges and perceptions, and ways to increase the value of generative AI through purposeful use. We have also created materials to provide students and instructors the necessary understanding to successfully navigate an AI-enabled world beyond the classroom. The team will share best practices, research findings, and suggestions for supporting implementation of generative AI in productive ways.

Human-Centered Intelligence: Designing an Institution-Wide AI Literacy Ecosystem

Miércoles 28 de octubre · 4:00 PM · 1 material (solicite acceso a SharePoint)
Rice University
Descripción

Colleges and universities have moved quickly to introduce AI tools, workshops, and guidance, but most efforts remain fragmented. In our research on R1 institutions “AI guidance versus AI policy” documentation, we’ve observed that faculty attend standalone sessions, students encounter inconsistent expectations (and transparency about those expectations) across courses. There are limited tutoring supports, and staff offering those supports receive uneven training tied to specific tools rather than transferable practices. Institutions often respond to immediate pressures rather than building durable systems. Shared governance models rely on deliberation and consensus-building, while AI adoption often moves speedily through vendor cycles, IT deployments, and informal faculty experimentation. Efforts to centralize can be read as overreach or loss of autonomy. Rice University has taken a different approach by initiating a coordinated ( not centralized ) AI literacy ecosystem that connects governance, policy, training, pedagogy, and infrastructure. This session introduces that architecture and the design decisions behind it. Participants will examine a four-pillar strategy and leave with a framework for moving beyond workshops toward a coordinated system that supports faculty agency, student learning, and institutional alignment. The session addresses a current challenge in higher education: how to move from experimentation to shared practice without losing flexibility or trust.

Opening Up the University: How a Library-College Partnership Built an AI Literacy Hub That Serves an Entire Institution

Miércoles 28 de octubre · 4:00 PM · 2 materiales (solicite acceso a SharePoint)
University of Virginia Library
Descripción

Students at every university use AI daily, largely invisible to instructors. Faculty, professional schools, career services, and community partners all face the same challenge in isolation. AI literacy develops by accident rather than by design. The University of Virginia built a different model. The UVA Library and the College of Arts & Sciences launched the AI Literacy and Action Lab (AILA) as a university-wide hub grounded in a published AI Literacy Framework (Lo, 2025). The Lab embeds trained librarian facilitators in faculty courses across disciplines, connects student consulting teams with community organizations, partners with career services on workforce readiness, and opens pathways for professional schools to join. Every engagement produces pre/post learning data, portfolio artifacts, and a published case study. This session presents the framework and hub model, shares evidence from pilots across the arts, humanities, social sciences, and sciences, and guides participants through a design activity. Participants leave with a plan to build a similar hub at their own institution.

From Equations to Exploration: Using Generative AI to Build Instructor-Verified Simulations for Active Learning

Miércoles 28 de octubre · 4:25 PM · 1 material (solicite acceso a SharePoint)
Rowan University
Descripción

Generative AI has rapidly become a concern in higher education, but it is also a tool that lets individual instructors build things they previously could not. This session demonstrates how faculty without programming backgrounds can use generative AI to create custom interactive simulations aligned to their own learning objectives, such as transforming static equations and relationships into hands-on exploratory tools students can manipulate to build intuition. I share a worked case from a biomechanical engineering context: a jaw-torque simulator built with Claude in a single working session, in which students vary joint angle, moment arm distance, and applied force and observe the resulting torque in real time. The session generalizes the workflow to any discipline in which student understanding depends on seeing how variables relate, as in economics, statistics, chemistry, biology, and beyond. The hope is that this represents an innovative teaching practice that strengthens meaningful learning: instructors' subject-matter expertise serves as the quality-assurance mechanism, as faculty can verify AI outputs against known analytical solutions before the tool reaches students. The session addresses prompting strategies, verification workflows, equity considerations around access, and common failure modes. It closes with participants drafting their own specifications for tools they could build after the session.

The Product Is Dead, Long Live the Process: Reimagining Assessment in the Age of AI

Miércoles 28 de octubre · 4:25 PM · 1 material (solicite acceso a SharePoint)
Eastern Kentucky University
Descripción

Generative AI has exposed a structural weakness in how higher education defines and enforces academic integrity. The dominant response has been predictable: adopt detection tools, restrict AI use, and increase surveillance. These approaches create the appearance of control but do little to produce trustworthy evidence of learning, and can further erode trust in the value of higher education institutions. This session reframes academic integrity as a design problem. When assessment depends primarily on evaluating a finished product, faculty have limited evidence of how students arrived there. Generative AI makes that limitation increasingly difficult to ignore. The result is a growing gap between policy expectations and what faculty can realistically verify. A more durable, workforce-ready approach shifts attention from products to process. Process-centered assessment makes student thinking visible through drafts, reflections, revision histories, and decision logs. These designs generate evidence of learning as it happens, reducing reliance on detection and lowering the risk of false accusations while developing transferable AI literacy skills students need. Drawing on faculty development work at Eastern Kentucky University, this session presents a practical framework for redesigning assignments to make learning legible, assessable, and defensible. Participants will be able to answer a more productive question: What evidence demonstrates that learning occurred?

Building AI Literacy for Every Student: MTSU's DATA 1010 as a General Education Gateway

Miércoles 28 de octubre · 5:00 PM · 1 material (solicite acceso a SharePoint)
Middle Tennessee State University
Descripción

How can a regional comprehensive university ensure every graduate—regardless of major—is prepared to think critically about artificial intelligence? Middle Tennessee State University answered that question by creating DATA 1010: Artificial Intelligence in Action, a 4-credit general education course developed by the AI Initiative, an interdisciplinary faculty community spanning business, computing, humanities, and the sciences. This session describes how DATA 1010 was designed to fulfill MTSU's True Blue Core scientific literacy outcomes, asking students to systematically investigate AI through the collection and analysis of evidence, to break down complex sociotechnical problems, and to draw informed conclusions. The course moves students through seven units—from the history of AI and large language models to multimodal systems, philosophy and ethics, law and policy, and a capstone AI research project—blending hands-on use of AI tools with rigorous inquiry into their societal impact. Presenters will share the collaborative design process behind the course, lessons learned from launch, and a replicable model for institutions seeking to embed AI literacy into general education without sacrificing disciplinary rigor.

TEACH-AI: Building a Collaborative Institutional Hub for Responsible AI Integration in Teaching and Learning

Miércoles 28 de octubre · 5:00 PM · 2 materiales (solicite acceso a SharePoint)
Clemson University
Descripción

This panel explores how a large research institution developed a sustainable, cross-campus model for engaging faculty and staff in the evolving higher education AI landscape. Emerging from the 2025–26 AAC&U Institute on AI, Pedagogy, and the Curriculum, our Clemson University team launched TEACH-AI, a central hub designed to foster dialogue, experimentation, and informed practice around AI in teaching. Initially led by the Clemson Libraries and the Office of Teaching Effectiveness and Innovation, the initiative quickly grew to units such as the Career Innovation Lab and faculty partners. The session highlights our move from the Institute to a scalable model with a distributed network of college representatives and faculty members. Panelists will share perspectives on building trust, coordinating across units, building strategic partnerships, and sustaining momentum while navigating the opportunities and tensions of AI adoption. They will address how a ground-up initiative can bring together diverse support for faculty. Attendees will gain insight into creating collaborative structures that support responsible, inclusive, and forward-looking AI integration.
